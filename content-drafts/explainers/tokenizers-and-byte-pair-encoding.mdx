A language model asked how many times the letter *r* appears in "strawberry" will often say two. This gets shared as evidence that the model does not really understand anything, and that reading is fair enough as far as it goes. But the more precise explanation is more interesting and more useful: the model was never shown the letters. It was shown two or three integers, and nothing in those integers records that one of them contains two *r*s.

The tokenizer is the least glamorous component in the stack and the source of a startling share of its weirdness. It is worth understanding on its own terms.

## 1. The problem tokenization solves

A model needs a finite vocabulary, because the output layer is a probability distribution over that vocabulary and its size is a fixed dimension of the network. So text has to become a bounded set of symbols. There are two obvious ways to do it, and both fail.

**Words.** Natural and useless. Vocabulary is unbounded — every proper noun, typo, compound and neologism is a new word — so you cap it and everything beyond the cap becomes `<UNK>`. The model then cannot read, write or reason about any rare word, which includes most of the words that carry the actual information in a technical document.

**Characters.** Bounded and complete: nothing is out of vocabulary, ever. But sequences become five to ten times longer, and attention cost is quadratic in sequence length. You have also handed the model the job of relearning that `c-a-t` is a unit, spending capacity on something you could have told it for free.

The escape is to make the vocabulary *learned* rather than chosen — frequent sequences become single tokens, rare ones decompose into pieces. Common text is short; unusual text is still representable. Nothing is ever out of vocabulary.

## 2. Byte pair encoding, borrowed from compression

BPE was published by Philip Gage in 1994 as a data-compression method. Rico Sennrich, Barry Haddow and Alexandra Birch adapted it for neural machine translation in *Neural Machine Translation of Rare Words with Subword Units*, presented at ACL 2016, and it has been the dominant approach ever since.

The training algorithm is short enough to state completely:

1. Start with the vocabulary as individual characters (or bytes).
2. Count every adjacent pair in the corpus.
3. Merge the most frequent pair into a single new symbol; add it to the vocabulary.
4. Repeat until the vocabulary reaches the target size.

That is all of it. Run on English, the early merges produce `th`, `he`, `in`, `er`; a few thousand merges later you have whole common words and productive suffixes like `ing`, `tion`, `ness`.

The output is an **ordered list of merge rules**, and the order is the algorithm. Encoding new text means applying those rules in the same sequence they were learned. The tokenizer is not a dictionary lookup; it is a replay of a training history.

### Watching it run

Take a tiny corpus: `low low low lower lowest`. Split into characters, with a marker for word ends:

```text
l o w _   l o w _   l o w _   l o w e r _   l o w e s t _
```

Count adjacent pairs. `l o` appears five times, more than anything else, so merge it:

```text
lo w _   lo w _   lo w _   lo w e r _   lo w e s t _
```

Now `lo w` appears five times. Merge:

```text
low _   low _   low _   low e r _   low e s t _
```

Then `low _` — the complete word — appears three times and becomes one token, while `e r _` and `e s t _` eventually merge into suffix tokens. After five merges the vocabulary contains `low`, `low_`, `er_`, `est_` and the original characters.

Notice what the algorithm discovered without being told: a stem and two suffixes. Nobody encoded morphology; it fell out of frequency counting. That is why BPE generalises — a word it has never seen, `lowness`, still decomposes into `low` + something rather than into unrelated fragments.

Notice also what it did **not** discover. The merges are driven purely by co-occurrence counts, so they are only as good as the corpus. A stem that is rare in training gets no merges and stays fragmented forever, no matter how meaningful it is.

<Feedback variant="info" title="Why byte-level BPE is now standard">
Starting from raw bytes rather than Unicode characters gives a base vocabulary of exactly 256 and makes the tokenizer total: any byte sequence in any language, plus emoji, plus binary, encodes without an unknown token. GPT-2 introduced this and essentially every model since has followed.
</Feedback>

## 3. Why "strawberry" defeats it

Run a typical tokenizer over `strawberry` and you get something like `str` + `aw` + `berry` — three integers. The model receives roughly `[2536, 707, 15717]`.

Now consider what counting *r*s would require. The model must know that token 2536 contains one *r*, that 707 contains none, and that 15717 contains two — facts that exist nowhere in the input. There is no character-level structure in an integer. The model can only know spelling to the extent that it has *memorised*, during training, which tokens contain which letters, from text that happened to discuss spelling.

It does memorise some of this, which is why the failure is inconsistent rather than total. But it is memorised trivia, not perception, and it degrades exactly where you would expect: rare words, unusual capitalisation, and words whose tokenization varies with surrounding whitespace.

The same mechanism explains several other reliable failures. **Reversing a string** requires character access the model does not have. **Arithmetic** is erratic partly because numbers tokenize inconsistently — `1234` might be one token while `1235` is two, so the model cannot rely on positional structure. **Rhyming** in some languages is hard because rhyme is a property of final phonemes and tokens do not align to them.

## 4. Tokenization is a fairness problem

Vocabularies are learned from a corpus, and corpora are mostly English. The consequences are concrete and rarely mentioned in model cards.

English text runs at roughly four characters per token. Languages written in other scripts fare far worse — the merges that would compress them were never learned, so text falls back toward per-character or per-byte tokens, sometimes several tokens per character.

Three real costs follow, all of them borne by non-English users:

- **Money.** APIs bill per token. The same sentence costs multiples more in Hindi, Thai or Burmese than in English.
- **Context.** A fixed context window holds far less actual content.
- **Quality.** Longer sequences mean more positions between related words, and attention has more work to do to connect them.

None of this is a deliberate design decision. It is an emergent property of where the training text came from, which is exactly why it goes unnoticed.

## 5. The operational details that bite

**Whitespace is inside the token.** In most modern tokenizers `" the"` and `"the"` are different tokens. This is why a prompt ending in a trailing space can produce noticeably worse output — you have forced the model to continue from a token that rarely appears in that position. Do not end prompts with a space.

**Token counts are not portable.** Every model family has its own vocabulary. A prompt that is 1,000 tokens for one model may be 1,300 for another. Cost and context estimates must use the tokenizer of the model you are actually calling.

**Structured formats are expensive.** JSON spends tokens on braces, quotes and repeated field names, and those tokens are billed and occupy context like any other. A response format with short field names is meaningfully cheaper than one with descriptive ones, at scale.

**The vocabulary is frozen at pre-training.** Terminology invented after the cutoff decomposes into awkward fragments, and the model must handle it without ever having had a dedicated symbol for it.

<Feedback variant="warning" title="Never estimate tokens by dividing characters by four">
The four-characters-per-token rule of thumb is an average over English prose. It is badly wrong for code, for JSON, for other scripts, and for anything with unusual formatting. Use the real tokenizer; every provider ships one and it takes two lines to call.
</Feedback>

## 6. BPE is not the only option

Three alternatives are in wide use, and the differences are worth knowing because they change the failure modes.

**WordPiece**, used by BERT, differs from BPE in the selection criterion. Rather than merging the most frequent pair, it merges the pair that most increases the likelihood of the training data under a unigram language model — roughly, it prefers pairs whose joint frequency is high *relative to* the product of their individual frequencies. In practice this favours merges that are genuinely a unit rather than merges of two independently common pieces.

**Unigram**, from Kudo, works backwards. Start with a large candidate vocabulary and iteratively *remove* the tokens whose deletion hurts the corpus likelihood least, until the target size is reached. Because it keeps a probability for every token, it can produce several valid segmentations of the same string with associated likelihoods — which enables **subword regularisation**, sampling different segmentations during training as a data-augmentation strategy.

**SentencePiece** is often listed alongside these but is a different kind of thing: an implementation that runs BPE or Unigram directly on raw text, treating the space character as an ordinary symbol (usually rendered `▁`). Its real contribution is removing the pre-tokenization step. Traditional pipelines split on whitespace first, which quietly assumes a language that uses whitespace — false for Chinese, Japanese and Thai. SentencePiece is language-agnostic and reversible: decoding returns the original string exactly, including its spacing.

<Feedback variant="info" title="Reversibility is not a given">
Older pipelines that lower-cased, stripped accents or normalised whitespace before tokenizing cannot reconstruct the input. For a model that generates text, that is a correctness problem, not an aesthetic one — you cannot faithfully echo a user's string you were never shown.
</Feedback>

## 7. What happens if we remove it

There is active work on making tokenization disappear — models that read raw bytes and learn their own chunking, sometimes dynamically, rather than inheriting a fixed merge table.

The appeal is obvious: no vocabulary bias, no spelling blindness, no cross-language cost asymmetry, no frozen terminology. The obstacle is equally obvious and is the same one from section 1: byte sequences are long, and attention is quadratic. That is why this line of work tends to appear alongside architectures with better long-sequence behaviour — the two problems are the same problem.

For now, tokenization remains, and so does its long tail of consequences.

## 8. The framing that makes this useful

The tokenizer is a lossy compression scheme sitting between your text and the model, and it was optimised for average sequence length on a mostly-English corpus — not for preserving the properties you might want.

Read the failure list again with that in mind and it stops being a list of quirks. Spelling, character counting, reversal, digit arithmetic, cross-language cost, prompt-boundary sensitivity: every one is a direct consequence of a compression decision made before the model saw a single example.

The model is not failing to count. It is answering a question about letters using an input that does not contain any.
