Consensus is what you need when several machines must agree on a sequence of decisions and any of them might crash, restart with stale state, or be cut off by a network partition at the least convenient moment. Every strongly-consistent distributed system contains one — etcd, Consul, CockroachDB, TiKV, Kafka's newer controller — and all of them exist to answer a question that sounds trivial until you try it: what, exactly, happened, and in what order?

Paxos solved this correctly in 1989 and has been famously difficult ever since. Diego Ongaro and John Ousterhout's contribution at Stanford was to observe that a correct algorithm nobody can implement confidently is a partial solution, and to design for understandability as an explicit goal. Their paper is titled *In Search of an Understandable Consensus Algorithm*, and it appeared at USENIX ATC in 2014.

The best way to see why Raft looks the way it does is to try to design it yourself and watch each obvious idea fail.

## 1. First attempt: just write to all of them

Send every write to all five servers. Wait for all five to acknowledge.

This is correct and useless. Availability is now *worse* than a single machine — five servers that must all respond fail more often than one, and you have added latency while subtracting reliability. Any single crash stops the system entirely.

The fix is **majority quorums**. Require agreement from more than half, so a five-node cluster tolerates two failures. The property that makes this work is that any two majorities of the same set must overlap in at least one member — so a decision made by one majority cannot be forgotten by a later one, because someone in the second group was in the first.

Everything else in Raft is machinery to exploit that overlap correctly.

## 2. Second attempt: let anyone accept writes

Any server accepts a write and propagates it to a majority.

This breaks immediately. Two clients write to different servers concurrently; each reaches a different majority; the two orderings disagree. Now you have two logs claiming to be the truth, and no way to reconcile them without discarding somebody's committed data.

Raft's response is decisive and is the single decision that makes it comprehensible: **one leader at a time**, and all writes go through it. The leader assigns the order. Followers accept it.

This trades some theoretical elegance for an enormous simplification. There is exactly one place where ordering is decided, so ordering conflicts cannot arise.

## 3. Third attempt: elect a leader by asking

Nodes vote. Whoever gets the most votes leads.

This fails on the partition case, which is the case that matters. A network split leaves two halves, each electing a leader, each accepting writes — the **split brain** that consensus exists to prevent.

Raft closes it with two mechanisms working together.

**Terms.** Time is divided into numbered terms, each with at most one leader. Every message carries its sender's term. A server seeing a higher term than its own immediately steps down and updates — so an old leader that was partitioned away and returns discovers it is stale on its very first exchange and defers without any special reconciliation logic.

**Majority votes, with a majority-sized restriction.** A candidate needs votes from more than half the cluster, and each server votes at most once per term. Since two majorities overlap, two candidates cannot both win the same term. A minority partition can hold an election, but it cannot reach a majority, so it simply cannot elect anyone — and correctly becomes unavailable rather than incorrectly available.

<Feedback variant="info" title="Unavailability is the correct behaviour">
The minority side of a partition stops serving writes. That is not a bug being tolerated; it is the design working. A system that keeps accepting writes on both sides of a partition is not consistent, and the choice between the two is what CAP is actually about.
</Feedback>

## 4. Fourth attempt: elections that never finish

If every server starts an election the moment it stops hearing from the leader, they all become candidates simultaneously, split the vote evenly, and nobody wins — repeatedly.

Raft's answer is almost embarrassingly simple: **randomised election timeouts**. Each server waits a random duration in a range — typically 150–300 ms — before starting an election. One server almost always times out first, requests votes, and wins before the others wake up.

It is not a proof of termination, it is a probabilistic argument, and it works reliably in practice. It is also a good example of the paper's philosophy: a simple mechanism that is easy to reason about, chosen over a cleverer one that is not.

## 5. The three server states

Raft servers are in exactly one of three states, which is the whole state machine:

- **Follower** — passive. Responds to leaders and candidates. All servers start here.
- **Candidate** — has timed out and is soliciting votes.
- **Leader** — handles all client requests and drives replication.

A follower that stops hearing heartbeats becomes a candidate. A candidate that wins becomes leader. Any server that sees a higher term reverts to follower. That is the entire lifecycle.

The paper decomposes the problem into three subproblems and treats them separately, which is itself much of the reason it is followable: **leader election**, **log replication**, and **safety**.

## 6. Log replication, and the matching property

Each entry in the log holds a command, an index, and the term in which it was created. The leader appends locally, then sends `AppendEntries` to followers.

The mechanism that keeps logs consistent is a small consistency check with a large consequence. Every `AppendEntries` includes the index and term of the entry immediately *preceding* the new ones. A follower rejects the request unless it has an entry matching that index and term.

From this follows the **Log Matching Property**: if two logs contain an entry with the same index and term, then the logs are identical in all entries up to that point. One induction step, and it means agreeing on a single position implies agreement on the entire prefix.

When a follower rejects, the leader decrements its guess of where they agree and retries, walking backwards until it finds the last common entry, then overwriting everything after it. Followers do not negotiate; the leader's log is authoritative, and conflicting follower entries are discarded.

An entry is **committed** once it is stored on a majority. At that point it is permanent, and the leader tells followers the commit index on subsequent messages so they can apply it to their state machines.

## 7. The safety restriction that is easy to miss

There is one more rule, and it is the one that most naive implementations get wrong.

Suppose a leader commits an entry to a majority, then crashes. A server that was not in that majority — and so lacks the entry — becomes a candidate. If it wins, it would overwrite committed data.

Raft prevents this in the **voting rule**: a server refuses its vote to any candidate whose log is less up to date than its own, where "more up to date" means a higher last term, or an equal last term and a longer log.

Because a committed entry is on a majority, and a winning candidate needs a majority, the two sets must overlap. That overlapping server holds the committed entry and will refuse to vote for a candidate lacking it. **A candidate missing committed data cannot win.**

<Feedback variant="warning" title="A leader may not commit an old entry by counting replicas">
A subtle related rule: a new leader must not consider an entry from a *previous* term committed merely because it is now on a majority — that situation can still be reversed. It commits an entry from its own term, and the Log Matching Property carries the earlier entries along with it. This is the corner where hand-rolled implementations most often go wrong.
</Feedback>

## 8. The parts the abstract skips

**Snapshots.** A log that only grows eventually exhausts disk and makes restart arbitrarily slow. Servers periodically snapshot their state machine and discard the prefix. A follower too far behind receives a snapshot instead of entries.

**Membership changes.** Adding and removing servers changes what a majority *is*, and switching directly risks a moment where two different majorities exist. Raft handles it with joint consensus — a transitional configuration requiring majorities of both old and new — or, more commonly in practice, by changing one server at a time, which cannot create disjoint majorities.

**Read consistency.** Reads seem safe from the leader, but a partitioned leader that does not yet know it has been replaced would serve stale data. Real systems confirm leadership before answering — via a heartbeat round or a lease — and this is a routine source of bugs in reimplementations.

## 9. Why understandability was the right goal

Raft is not faster than Paxos and does not tolerate more failures. It offers the same guarantees. Its advantage is entirely that engineers can hold it in their heads, and that turned out to matter more than any performance property — because a consensus implementation that its maintainers do not fully understand is a liability regardless of its asymptotics.

The paper backed the claim with an unusual experiment: they taught both algorithms to students and quizzed them, and Raft scored significantly higher. Treating comprehensibility as a measurable design objective rather than a matter of taste is, I think, the most transferable thing about the work.

It is also the reason essentially every consensus system built since 2014 chose Raft. The correctness was available in 1989. The ability to implement it confidently was not.
